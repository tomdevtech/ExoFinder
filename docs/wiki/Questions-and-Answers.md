# Questions and answers

## About the planets

**What is an exoplanet?**
A planet that circles a star other than our Sun.

**Are the pictures of exoplanets real photos?**
No. No telescope can see the surface of an exoplanet. ExoFinder computes each picture from the measured values: a large, cold planet becomes an icy gas giant, a small, hot one becomes a glowing rock. The planets of our solar system are the exception. They show real maps made from spacecraft images.

**What do "rocky planet", "Super-Earth", "Neptune-like planet" and "gas giant" mean?**
These are the four kinds of planets. ExoFinder sorts them by how wide they are, compared with Earth:

| Kind | Width | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rocky planet | less than 1.25 × Earth | made of rock and metal, like Earth or Mars |
| Super-Earth | 1.25 to 2 × Earth | bigger than Earth, but smaller than Neptune |
| Neptune-like planet | 2 to 6 × Earth | with a thick layer of gas, like Neptune |
| Gas giant | more than 6 × Earth | mostly gas, like Jupiter and Saturn |

If only the weight of a planet is known, ExoFinder estimates its width from the weight. If neither is known, the planet is not sorted and is simply called "Planet".

**What do "frozen", "cold", "temperate", "hot" and "scorching" mean?**
They describe how warm the planet is from its starlight alone (the equilibrium temperature on the planet page):

| Word | Temperature |
| --- | --- |
| frozen | below −123 °C |
| cold | −123 °C to −23 °C |
| temperate | −23 °C to 77 °C |
| hot | 77 °C to 727 °C |
| scorching | above 727 °C |

"Temperate" does not mean that someone could live there. An atmosphere can make a planet much warmer, as on Venus.

**Why is a value missing?**
It has not been measured yet. Many planets were found with a method that tells only their size, or only their weight. A missing value shows **unknown**.

**What does "calculated" next to a value mean?**
The value was worked out from other values instead of being measured. For example, the density follows from size and weight.

**What does "minimum mass" mean?**
Some methods tell only the lowest possible weight of a planet. The real weight may be higher.

## About the data

**Where does the data come from?**
The exoplanets come from the [NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/). The planets of our solar system come from the [NASA Planetary Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/).

**How up to date is it?**
As up to date as the last comparison with NASA. The start page says when that was. New planets appear on their own, at the latest an hour after the comparison: until then a page may still show the earlier state. Changed values are recorded in the change history of the planet page.

**A planet has a notice that NASA no longer lists it. Why?**
Sometimes a discovery turns out to be wrong, or NASA takes a planet off its list for another reason. Its page stays in ExoFinder, with the last known values.

**I followed an old link and landed on a page with another name.**
The planet or star was renamed. Old links lead to the new page, and the page lists the former name.

## About the map

**The map stays black or shows a message about WebGL 2.**
Your browser or your computer does not support WebGL 2, or it is turned off. Update your browser, or try another one. The planet and star pages work without it, only the 3D parts are missing.

**Why are some stars missing from the map?**
The map shows only stars with known planets. Stars whose distance is not known cannot be placed and are left out. Their star page tells you so.

**Why do the planets look so big in the system view?**
So that you can see them. With the real sizes and distances, every planet would be smaller than a pixel.

**I am lost on the map.**
Click **Sun** in the top right corner to fly back home, or **Whole galaxy** to see the whole Milky Way and turn it round its centre.
